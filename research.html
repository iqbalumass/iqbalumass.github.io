Research

Secure and Trustworthy Multimodal Intelligence

My research focuses on making AI systems more reliable when they work with images, language, and other sources of information. I study how these systems fail when their data is manipulated or incomplete, and how we can detect and correct those failures. My work connects computer vision, vision-language models, and adversarial machine learning with image generation and cooperative reasoning for connected vehicles.

Detecting and Repairing Backdoors

In our work on poisoned CLIP models, we use an image-segmentation model as a reference to help identify potential triggers, affected labels, and victim samples. We use these findings to construct a small dataset for corrective fine-tuning. The central idea is to connect attack detection with model repair: what we learn about an attack guides the data we use to correct it.

This work led to my first-authored paper in Multimedia Tools and Applications. I also co-authored a WACV 2025 paper on black-box poisoning of exemplar-based class-incremental learning models and a first-authored survey of adversarial attacks and defenses on vision-language models in Neural Networks.

Reliable Retrieval-Augmented Image Generation

Robust Vision RAG · Under review at The Visual Computer (Springer)

Retrieved images can help a diffusion model follow a user’s request, but poisoned examples can steer the output toward misleading content. We developed a semantic-coherence refinement method that checks agreement between retrieved images and the prompt, refines misaligned parts of the prompt, and retrieves revised examples before generation. The study examines resistance to retrieval poisoning alongside visual quality and alignment with the user’s intended content.

Cooperative Reasoning for Connected Vehicles

Ongoing research

I am developing a Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) framework in which a roadside unit combines vehicle and roadside observations into an occlusion-aware scene graph. The graph records road users, visibility, occlusions, and potential conflicts. A language model uses these relationships to identify hidden hazards and explain its recommendations.

For example, a following vehicle may see a cyclist behind a stopped bus while a turning vehicle cannot. Shared observations connect that hidden cyclist to the turning vehicle’s potential conflict. My evaluation plan uses scene representations derived from V2X-Real annotations and synthetic scenarios, comparing pretrained and fine-tuned models on hazard identification, recommended actions, and explanations grounded in graph evidence. This work focuses on reasoning and decision support; vehicle control requires separate validation.

Future Direction: Reasoning from Shared Observations

I plan to study how cooperative AI should respond when observations disagree, arrive late, or may have been corrupted. Recording an observation’s source, time, and uncertainty can help a recommendation reflect the strength of its evidence. A key challenge is distinguishing an attack from an ordinary difference in what two sensors can see.

My initial work will use public datasets, pretrained models, parameter-efficient adaptation, and comparisons with rule-based methods. Longer-term questions include when a system should request more information or defer a decision.

Student Research Opportunities

I plan to involve undergraduate and master’s students in dataset analysis, visualization, baseline implementation, and model evaluation. Students would begin with manageable tasks and clear milestones, then develop focused questions they can investigate and defend. My industry experience informs my attention to reproducibility, software quality, and practical constraints.
