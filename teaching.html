Teaching

My goal is to help students become independent problem solvers who understand why an algorithm works, connect computational concepts to practical problems, and evaluate the evidence supporting their solutions. My teaching approach combines active learning, scaffolding, and Universal Design for Learning (UDL).

Examples from My Teaching Work

Making algorithm growth tangible

In CIS 360, I used a lab in which students ran the same algorithm on small and much larger arrays. The change in execution time provided a starting point for discussing how input size affects an algorithm’s practicality. In future courses, I would extend this activity by asking students to predict, run, and explain, then connect timing observations to operation counts and complexity.

Exploring the closest-pair problem

As a graduate TA for CIS 522, I created assignments and graded submissions, quizzes, midterms, and final examinations. One assignment used a game-like airplane collision detection setting to explore the closest-pair problem and divide-and-conquer reasoning. In future courses, I would guide students through a baseline solution, then reduce support as they investigate boundary cases and justify a more efficient approach.

How I Plan to Support Learning

  • Active learningStudents predict outcomes, investigate examples, and explain the reasoning behind their solutions.
  • ScaffoldingWorked examples and guiding questions provide an initial foundation; support decreases as students become more independent.
  • Universal Design for LearningRunnable code, labeled diagrams, and written explanations offer complementary ways into a concept while preserving shared expectations for implementation and analysis.
  • Feedback and reflectionRubrics, written reasoning, and office-hour conversations help students identify a concrete next step. Student feedback, assessed work, and peer observation will guide improvements to my teaching.

Connecting Coursework to Practice

My industry experience informs projects that include testing, documentation, code review, and an explanation of design choices. In advanced AI courses, I would use manageable datasets to help students examine model failures and unsupported outputs. Where AI assistance is permitted, I would require disclosure and ask students to explain and verify their work.
